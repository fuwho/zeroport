
  ##########################################################################
    Z E R O P O R T      a guided walkthrough
  
    A network where nothing listens on the internet — and authorized
    peers still connect, at full speed, with every flow attributed.
  ##########################################################################

    Running on loopback: 127.0.0.1
    Five processes, each a separate program, talking over real sockets.

       directory   — who exists and what they may reach   127.0.0.1:8801
       rendezvous  — how two peers find each other        127.0.0.1:8802
       finance-db      the protected service    zp16989075c6a
       officer-laptop  an authorized officer    zp16cf61eab13
       unknown-device  no invitation            zp1ccfcfca5af

       The two legitimate machines have been enrolled by two officers.

  ==========================================================================
  STEP 1 of 6   THE DOOR THAT ISN'T THERE
  ==========================================================================

  Every attack starts the same way: find something that answers.
  A firewall answers. A VPN gateway answers. A bastion answers. That is
  how they get found, mapped, and eventually broken into.
  
  So look for the service the way an attacker would.

   > Scanning 127.0.0.1 for anything that will accept a connection...
       37 ports probed. The database port 5432: nothing there.
       The agent's own socket 57367: refuses TCP entirely.
       (8801 and 8802 are the directory and rendezvous, not the service.)
   > Firing a packet straight at the agent, without credentials...
       No reply. The agent read it, found no valid signature, and stopped.
       An attacker learns nothing — not even that something is there.
   > Now the officer's laptop connects to the same machine...
       Connected. Encrypted round trip returned "ACK:SELECT 1" in 2.86 ms.

  ── The idea:  the service is not hidden behind a lock — it has no door at 
                all. Being able to reach it is a property of holding the 
                right key, which is why the scanner and the officer can both 
                be telling the truth. 

  ==========================================================================
  STEP 2 of 6   HOW TWO MACHINES MEET WHEN NEITHER CAN BE CALLED
  ==========================================================================

  Both machines sit behind their own network. Neither has a public address,
  so neither can dial the other. They need an introduction.
  
  A rendezvous performs that introduction — and then gets out of the way.
  Watch the same sealed packet travel both routes.

   > Sending it the long way, through the rendezvous...
       Two hops — laptop to rendezvous, rendezvous to service. 20.5 ms.
       The introducer moved the bytes but could not read them.
   > Now the same packet, directly...
       One hop. 2.2 ms. The introducer is no longer in the path.

  ── The idea:  the introduction is brief and the middleman leaves. Traffic 
                runs directly between the two machines, so there is no third 
                party in the middle of the conversation and nothing to slow 
                it down or subpoena. 

  ==========================================================================
  STEP 3 of 6   WHO IS ALLOWED TO CHANGE THE GUEST LIST
  ==========================================================================

  Someone has to decide which machines belong on the network. In most
  systems that is an administrator account — and an administrator account
  can be phished, coerced, or simply wrong.
  
  Here the authority to change the list is split across three officers.

   > One officer tries to add an unknown device on their own...
       The directory refuses it: "invalid: signature verification failed".
       Not a permission error. One share cannot form a signature for the group key,
       so the thing they tried to submit is not a valid signature at all.
   > A second officer signs the same list...
       Accepted. Two shares combined into one signature.
       A verifier sees a single ordinary signature; it cannot tell two people made it.
   > Putting the safe list back...
       Done.

  ── The idea:  no single administrator can quietly add a machine to this 
                network. Not because policy forbids it, but because the 
                signature one person is able to produce does not exist as a 
                valid signature. 

  ==========================================================================
  STEP 4 of 6   WHAT HAPPENS TO A LAPTOP THAT IS STOLEN
  ==========================================================================

  A device is taken. Nobody notices for a day. In most networks its
  credentials keep working until a human revokes them.
  
  Here, permission is a lease that has to be renewed — so the question
  becomes what happens when a device simply stops asking.

   > Giving the laptop a three-second lease...
       It connects normally.
   > Now four seconds pass. Nothing is revoked. Nobody touches anything...
       Refused.
       The service recorded why: "lease expired".

  ── The idea:  access decays unless it is renewed, so a device that goes 
                quiet loses its permission on its own. The safe state is the 
                default, and nobody has to be paying attention for it to 
                hold. 

  ==========================================================================
  STEP 5 of 6   THE TWO WAYS IN THAT DO NOT WORK
  ==========================================================================

  Two attempts every real network faces: a machine that was never invited,
  and an invited machine reaching for something it was never given.
  
  Then we take a machine off the list while it is connected.

   > The unknown device tries the database...
       Dropped in silence.
       The service's log says: "peer static key not on the signed roster".
   > The officer reaches for a port nobody granted her — SSH on 22...
       Dropped.
       Log: "no policy permits zp16cf61eab13 -> zp16989075c6a:22".
       Her permission is for one port on one machine, not for the network.
   > Now one officer removes her from the list...
       Accepted — signed by a single officer.
       Adding a machine takes two people. Removing one takes one, deliberately.
       She is gone from the network. Log: "peer revoked".

  ── The idea:  permission is per-machine and per-port, and it is checked on 
                every single request. A device that is not on the list, or 
                is reaching somewhere it should not, is refused without ever 
                learning why. 

  ==========================================================================
  STEP 6 of 6   THE RECORD OF WHAT HAPPENED
  ==========================================================================

  A security team's last line of defence is the log. Which is a problem,
  because whoever breaks in can usually edit it.
  
  Each entry here carries the fingerprint of the one before it, and the
  end of the chain is published somewhere the attacker does not control.

   > Reading back what the service recorded...
       #14  ALLOW  zp16cf61eab13 -> zp16989075c6a:5432  8 bytes
       #16  ALLOW  zp16cf61eab13 -> zp16989075c6a:5432  8 bytes
       #19  DENY   unknown — peer static key not on the signed roster
       #21  DENY   zp16cf61eab13 — no policy permits zp16cf61eab13 -> zp16989075c6a:22
       #24  DENY   zp16cf61eab13 — peer revoked
       Every line names a machine, not an IP address that has to be guessed at.
   > Publishing the end of the chain to an outside timestamp service...
       Accepted by a.pool.opentimestamps.org — a witness we do not control.
   > An intruder edits one line to hide a refusal...
       Caught at line 2 — the fingerprints stop matching.
   > So they replace the whole file with a clean one instead...
       The new file is internally consistent: yes.
       But it does not match the record published outside. Caught.

  ── The idea:  the log is not trusted because we promise to look after it. 
                Editing one line breaks the chain, and swapping the whole 
                file fails against a copy of the fingerprint held by 
                somebody else.

    What you just watched
  ##########################################################################

    Nothing listened, so there was nothing to find.
    Two machines that could not call each other were introduced, then
    spoke directly with the introducer out of the way.
    Adding a machine took two officers; removing one took a single officer.
    A quiet device lost its access without anyone intervening.
    Every request was checked against a named identity and a named port.
    And the record of it cannot be edited without that showing.
